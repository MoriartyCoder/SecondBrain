
Data augmentation in the Thermal Infrared (TIR) domain requires modeling radiometric physics, sensor electro-optics, and atmospheric propagation. Unlike visible-spectrum RGB imagery—where pixel values represent reflected ambient light dominated by surface chrominance and albedo—TIR sensors capture self-emitted thermal radiance modulated by surface temperature, emissivity, atmospheric attenuation, and detector focal plane array (FPA) response characteristics.

In counter-unmanned aerial vehicle (Anti-UAV) applications, targets such as micro- and mini-drones present unique sensing challenges. Drones possess small physical dimensions, non-rigid thermal signatures (hot motor nodes vs. cold airframes), and high mobility, often leading to dot-to-object transitions across wide slant ranges. When tracking algorithms are trained on benchmark datasets like Anti-UAV410, standard RGB spatial augmentations (e.g., random cropping, horizontal flipping, color jittering) fail to capture the physics of infrared image formation.

## Introduction and Radiometric Foundations in Anti-UAV Sensing

The Anti-UAV410 benchmark comprises 410 high-definition thermal infrared video sequences containing over 438,000 annotated frames. The benchmark systematically categorizes operational tracking challenges into six primary visual and radiometric attributes: Thermal Crossover (TC), Scale Variation (SV), Occlusion (OC), Out-of-View (OV), Dynamic Background Clutter (DBC), and Fast Motion (FM).

|**Metric**|**Scientific Definition & Mathematical Formulation**|**Operational Significance**|
|---|---|---|
|**State Accuracy (SA)**|$\text{SA} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \left[ \text{IoU}_t \cdot v_t + p_t \cdot (1 - v_t) \right]$, where $\text{IoU}_t$ is spatial overlap, $v_t \in \{0, 1\}$ is visibility flag, and $p_t \in \{0, 1\}$ is out-of-view prediction accuracy.|Evaluates joint localization overlap and presence verification when drones exit the field of view.|
|**Precision (P)**|Percentage of frames where target center location error $\sqrt{(x_{pred} - x_{gt})^2 + (y_{pred} - y_{gt})^2} \le 20\text{ pixels}$.|Measures spatial drift and pixel-level centroid localization accuracy.|
|**Success Rate (AUC)**|Area under the curve of the Intersection over Union (IoU) threshold plot spanned continuously across $[0, 1]$.|Quantifies scale adaptation and spatial bounding box alignment across sequence trajectories.|

State-of-the-art single-object tracking (SOT) frameworks on Anti-UAV410—such as SiamDT, Enhanced-SiamDT, OSGT, and MixFormerV2—utilize deep Siamese or Transformer-based backbone networks to extract spatial-temporal features. However, empirical evaluations demonstrate significant performance drops when encountering thermal crossover and tiny-scale targets.

Deep backbones trained on unaugmented TIR sequences tend to overfit to local intensity contrast gradients. When a target drone enters a thermal crossover state or experiences atmospheric attenuation at long range, these contrast gradients dissolve. The cross-correlation or self-attention mechanisms in SiamDT or Transformer trackers subsequently lose target saliency, leading to track drift or bounding box collapse.

## Theoretical Physics, Governing Formulations, and Simplification Rationales of Baseline Augmentations

### NETD Noise (Noise Equivalent Temperature Difference)

Noise Equivalent Temperature Difference ($NETD$) quantifies the minimum detectable temperature difference of a thermal sensor, corresponding to an optical signal-to-noise ratio ($SNR$) of unity. $NETD$ represents the temporal noise floor generated by detector photon shot noise, Johnson-Nyquist thermal noise within microbolometer resistors, $1/f$ flicker noise, and readout integrated circuit (ROIC) noise.

The governing radiometric formula for $NETD$ integrates system-level detector properties and spectral radiance derivatives:

$$NETD = \frac{v_n}{\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}} = \frac{2 \sqrt{\pi} F^2 \sqrt{A_d \cdot B}}{\tau_o \tau_a D_{\lambda_p}^* \int_{\lambda_1}^{\lambda_2} \frac{\partial L(\lambda, T)}{\partial T} d\lambda}$$

where $F$ is the optical F-number, $A_d$ is the active detector area, $B$ is the electrical noise bandwidth, $\tau_o$ and $\tau_a$ are optical and atmospheric transmittances, $D_{\lambda_p}^*$ is peak specific detectivity, and $\frac{\partial L(\lambda, T)}{\partial T}$ is the derivative of Planck's blackbody radiance curve with respect to temperature.

In deep learning data augmentation, $NETD$ noise is simplified to a heteroscedastic, signal-dependent Gaussian noise distribution superimposed over the image intensity array $I(x,y)$:

$$I_{aug}(x,y) = I(x,y) + \mathcal{N}\left(0, \sigma_{noise}^2(I)\right)$$

$$\sigma_{noise}(I) = \sigma_{base} \cdot \left( \frac{NETD}{\Delta T_{scene}} \right) \cdot \sqrt{\frac{I(x,y)}{I_{max}}}$$

where $\Delta T_{scene}$ represents the dynamic temperature span of the scene and $\sigma_{base}$ is normalized baseline intensity variance.

The physical rationale for this simplification stems from the fact that calculating temporal noise dynamics across individual microbolometer semiconductor energy bands demands microsecond-level thermal equilibrium integration, which is computationally prohibitive during online training. Scaling standard zero-mean Gaussian noise variance by the ratio of sensor $NETD$ to local thermal contrast preserves the operational signal-to-noise degradation characteristics while remaining executable in tensorized GPU operations.

### Thermal Crossover

Thermal crossover occurs during diurnal environmental cycles when solar absorption, ambient convection, and radiative cooling cause the surface temperature and radiometric emittance of a target ($L_{obj}$) to match that of its surrounding background ($L_{bkg}$).

The radiometric intensity contrast $C$ between target and background is expressed as:

$$C = a \cdot \left\vert{} \int_{\lambda_1}^{\lambda_2} \left( [1 - \text{BRDF}_{obj}(\lambda)] L_{black}(\lambda, T_{obj}) - [1 - \text{BRDF}_{bkg}(\lambda)] L_{black}(\lambda, T_{bkg}) \right) d\lambda \right\vert{}$$

where $\text{BRDF}$ denotes the directional spectral reflectance. Crossover occurs when $C \to 0$, causing structural target boundary signatures to disappear in the thermal spectrum.

Target thermal crossover is simplified in image processing by alpha-blending the target region $I_{target}$ with local background spatial statistics, accompanied by structural edge attenuation and optional intensity polarity reversal:

$$I_{target}^{aug}(x,y) = \alpha \cdot I_{target}(x,y) + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \mu_{bkg}(x,y) + \eta \cdot \nabla^2 I_{bkg}(x,y)$$

$$\alpha \sim \text{Uniform}(0.0, 0.35)$$

where $\mu_{bkg}$ is the localized spatial mean intensity and $\nabla^2 I_{bkg}$ injects local background texture into the decaying target region.

Computing 3D surface heat balances, thermodynamic transport equations, and BRDF spectral reflections requires high-resolution material models and weather telemetry unavailable in 2D tracking datasets. Local intensity blending combined with background texture injection simulates target contrast loss while forcing deep trackers to rely on spatio-temporal motion cues rather than intensity thresholding.

### Thermal Blooming

Thermal blooming occurs when intense infrared radiation (e.g., hot drone engine exhaust, friction-heated rotor hubs, solar reflections) exceeds detector well charge capacity or causes thermal conduction across adjacent microbolometer pixels ($C_{th}, R_{th}$), compounded by optical diffraction point spread functions (PSF).

Pixel-to-pixel thermal diffusion across microbolometer arrays is governed by the 2D heat conduction equation:

$$\frac{\partial T(x,y,t)}{\partial t} = \frac{k}{\rho c_p} \nabla^2 T(x,y,t) + \frac{Q_{absorbed}(x,y,t)}{C_{th}}$$

combined with the optical diffraction-limited PSF:

$$h_{opt}(r) = \left[ \frac{2 J_1(\pi r / \lambda F)}{\pi r / \lambda F} \right]^2$$

Thermal blooming is simplified into an intensity-conditioned anisotropic Gaussian expansion applied to high-radiance target regions:

$$I_{bloomed}(x,y) = I(x,y) + \gamma \cdot \left( \mathbf{M}_{high} * K_{\sigma(I)} \right)(x,y)$$

$$\mathbf{M}_{high}(x,y) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } I(x,y) \ge T_{threshold} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

$$K_{\sigma(I)}(x,y) = \exp\left( -\frac{x^2 + y^2}{2 (\sigma_0 + k \cdot I(x,y))^2} \right)$$

Solving non-linear transient partial differential equations for 2D microbolometer array heat transfer online introduces massive computational overhead. Threshold-activated anisotropic Gaussian blur kernels approximate optical diffraction wings and pixel bleeding around hot engine components in real time.

### Atmospheric Refraction and Turbulence

Spatial and temporal fluctuations in atmospheric air density, micro-temperature gradients, and humidity alter the local optical refractive index $n(r)$ along the line of sight. These variations create optical path length differences (OPD) that warp incoming thermal wavefronts.

The optical refractive index structure parameter $C_n^2$ dictates spatial phase variance across a receiver aperture of diameter $D$ over path length $L$:

$$D_n(r) = C_n^2 r^{2/3}$$

$$\sigma_{\phi}^2 = 2.914 k^2 C_n^2 L D^{5/3}$$

This phase variance results in localized centroid motion, spatial shimmer, and geometric distortion of aerial targets.

Atmospheric turbulence is simplified using low-frequency 2D elastic vector field deformations parameterized by spatial Thin-Plate Splines (TPS) or smooth Perlin noise fields:

$$(x', y') = (x, y) + \vec{\delta}(x, y)$$

$$\vec{\delta}(x, y) = \sum_{k=1}^K w_k \cdot \phi\left( \Vert{} (x,y) - C_k \Vert{} \right)$$

where $C_k$ are control points and $\phi(r) = r^2 \log r$ is the radial basis function.

Full physical modeling of optical turbulence requires split-step Fourier numerical wave propagation through randomized phase screens. Smooth, low-frequency vector spatial shifts replicate image shimmering, target shape jitter, and edge distortion without requiring wave-optics propagation math.

### Environmental and Seasonal Drift

Long-term atmospheric baseline shifts, seasonal vegetation changes, variations in relative humidity, and diurnal ambient temperature fluctuations ($T_{amb}$) shift overall scene radiance profiles and optical depth $\tau(s_1, s_2)$.

Total radiance reaching the sensor $L_{scene}$ integrates attenuated target radiance, path radiance, and reflected sky radiance:

$$L_{scene}(\lambda) = \tau_{atm}(\lambda) \cdot L_{obj}(\lambda, T_{obj}) + [1 - \tau_{atm}(\lambda)] \cdot L_{atm}(\lambda, T_{atm}) + \rho_{obj}(\lambda) \cdot L_{sky}(\lambda)$$

Environmental drift is simplified via non-linear contrast stretching, linear gain/offset shifts, and gamma transformations applied across global thermal dynamic ranges:

$$I_{drift}(x,y) = \text{Clamp}\left( g \cdot [I(x,y)]^\gamma + o, 0, 255 \right)$$

$$g \sim \text{Uniform}(0.7, 1.3), \quad o \sim \text{Uniform}(-30, 30), \quad \gamma \sim \text{Uniform}(0.6, 1.5)$$

Full radiative transfer codes (such as MODTRAN) require explicit atmospheric vertical profiles (e.g., aerosol extinction coefficients, water vapor columns). Direct non-linear gain, offset, and gamma transformations mimic global background temperature baseline shifts and camera auto-gain recalibration.

## Novel Physically Backed Data Augmentations for Thermal UAV Sensing

Existing TIR augmentations focus primarily on global intensity and target contrast adjustments. However, specialized infrared hardware artifacts and range-dependent atmospheric attenuation present significant operational failure modes. The following five novel, physically sound augmentations address these gaps.

### Microbolometer Non-Uniformity Correction (NUC) Drift & Fixed-Pattern Noise (FPN)

Uncooled microbolometer FPAs suffer from pixel-to-pixel variations in responsivity $R_{i,j}$ and bias offset $O_{i,j}$ due to semiconductor manufacturing tolerances. Although sensors apply Non-Uniformity Correction (NUC) via flat-field calibration, ambient operational temperature shifts cause NUC parameters to drift over time. This drift produces persistent vertical and horizontal stripe patterns across the sensor array.

The non-uniform response of raw FPA pixel $(i,j)$ is modeled as:

$$Y_{i,j}(t) = g_{i,j}(T_{sensor}) \cdot X_{i,j}(t) + o_{i,j}(T_{sensor}) + n_{i,j}(t)$$

$$\mathbf{S}_{FPN}(i,j) = U_i^{col} + V_j^{row} + W_{i,j}^{grid}$$

$$U_i^{col} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(0, \sigma_{col}^2\right), \quad V_j^{row} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(0, \sigma_{row}^2\right), \quad W_{i,j}^{grid} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(0, \sigma_{grid}^2\right)$$

Fixed-Pattern Noise is simplified by adding persistent, low-rank column-wise and row-wise noise matrices combined with high-frequency spatial gain maps to tensor batches:

$$I_{NUC}(i,j) = \text{Clamp}\left( I(i,j) \cdot (\mathbf{1} + \mathbf{G}_{FPN}(i,j)) + \mathbf{S}_{FPN}(i,j), 0, 255 \right)$$

where $\mathbf{S}_{FPN}$ remains static across temporal tracking sub-sequences (simulating persistent sensor calibration artifacts).

Modeling individual VOx (Vanadium Oxide) or a-Si (Amorphous Silicon) microbolometer temperature-dependent resistance curves requires proprietary sensor telemetry. Generating low-rank static matrix offsets combined with multiplicative gain maps accurately captures output stripe artifacts at negligible computational cost.

Small aerial targets ($<8\times8$ pixels on Anti-UAV410) are frequently lost when their bounding box overlaps with uncorrected vertical FPA stripes. Injecting synthetic FPN forces SiamDT and Transformer tracking backbones to uncouple target features from static vertical sensor patterns.

### Scale-Dependent Atmospheric Extinction & Path Radiance (Beer-Lambert Model)

Radiance emitted by an aerial target attenuates exponentially over slant range distance $d$ due to molecular absorption ($\text{CO}_2, \text{H}_2\text{O}$) and aerosol scattering (Mie scattering from fog/haze). Concurrently, atmospheric in-scattering introduces path radiance $L_{path}$, elevating background intensity and eroding target-to-background contrast.

Governed by the Beer-Lambert-Bouguer law and atmospheric transfer equations:

$$\tau_{atm}(d) = \exp\left( -\int_{0}^d \beta_{ext}(s) \, ds \right) = \exp(-\beta_{ext} \cdot d)$$

$$L_{observed}(d) = L_{target}^0 \cdot e^{-\beta_{ext} \cdot d} + L_{path} \cdot \left(1 - e^{-\beta_{ext} \cdot d}\right)$$

Because monocular tracking datasets lack explicit 3D range depth maps, target bounding box scale $S = \sqrt{w_{box} \cdot h_{box}}$ serves as a physical proxy for target distance ($d \propto 1 / S$). Target intensity within the bounding box is exponentially attenuated toward path radiance as scale decreases:

$$I_{target}^{aug}(x,y) = I_{target}(x,y) \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{\kappa_{ext}}{S_{norm}}\right) + I_{path} \cdot \left[ 1 - \exp\left(-\frac{\kappa_{ext}}{S_{norm}}\right) \right]$$

$$S_{norm} = \frac{\sqrt{w_{box} \cdot h_{box}}}{\sqrt{W_{image} \cdot H_{image}}}$$

Assuming bounding box area correlates inversely with distance provides a reliable geometric proxy for target slant range. This enables scale-dependent atmospheric attenuation without requiring 3D scene reconstruction or dense depth sensors.

Drones approaching or receding from defense perimeters undergo continuous "dot-to-object" transitions. Scale-dependent extinction training prevents deep detectors from expecting high-contrast thermal signatures on visually small targets, boosting tiny-target recall.

### Sensor Integration & Motion-Induced MTF Blur

Target agility combined with fast optical pan/tilt gimbal adjustments induces relative spatial motion during detector integration time $t_{int}$. This temporal integration smears target energy across neighboring pixels, degrading the modulation transfer function ($MTF_{motion}$).

The linear motion MTF in the spatial frequency domain ($u$) is expressed as:

$$MTF_{motion}(u) = \left\vert{} \frac{\sin(\pi u v t_{int})}{\pi u v t_{int}} \right\vert{} = \text{sinc}(\pi u v t_{int})$$

The corresponding spatial blur kernel $h_{motion}(x,y)$ along motion angle $\theta$ with velocity magnitude $L = v \cdot t_{int}$ is:

$$h_{motion}(x,y) = \frac{1}{L} \text{rect}\left( \frac{x \cos \theta + y \sin \theta}{L} \right) \delta(-x \sin \theta + y \cos \theta)$$

Spatial motion blur is simplified by convolving target search regions with a directional line kernel derived from frame-to-frame bounding box displacement vectors $(\Delta x, \Delta y)$:

$$\theta = \arctan2(\Delta y, \Delta x), \quad L = \text{Clamp}\left(\sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2} \cdot \eta_{scale}, 1, L_{max}\right)$$

$$I_{blur}(x,y) = \left( I * h_{motion}(\theta, L) \right)(x,y)$$

Deriving exact integration time parameters ($t_{int}$) requires low-level sensor firmware access. Calculating spatial motion displacement vectors directly from ground-truth bounding box trajectory histories provides an accurate, automated spatial blur model.

This augmentation directly targets tracking failures associated with the Fast Motion (FM) attribute on Anti-UAV410. It prevents tracking bounding box collapse during high-speed drone maneuvers.

### Aerodynamic Rotor Wash & Thermal Exhaust Plume Dynamics

Electric motors, lithium-polymer batteries, and internal combustion engines on UAV platforms release operational heat. Rapidly spinning rotor blades generate turbulent downwash air currents that pull hot motor exhaust into trailing aerodynamic thermal plumes.

The spatial temperature dispersion of trailing rotor downwash is modeled using 2D Gaussian plume equations:

$$C(x,y) = \frac{Q}{2\pi u \sigma_y \sigma_z} \exp\left( -\frac{y^2}{2\sigma_y^2} \right) \exp\left( -\frac{x^2}{2\sigma_x^2} \right)$$

where $Q$ is heat emission rate, $u$ is relative wind/flight velocity, and $\sigma_y, \sigma_z$ are directional turbulent dispersion coefficients.

An asymmetric, elliptical thermal tail is simplified behind the target bounding box along the trailing motion vector $-\vec{v}_{uav}$:

$$I_{plume}(x,y) = I(x,y) + A_{plume} \cdot \exp\left( -\left[ \frac{x_{rot}^2}{2\sigma_{major}^2} + \frac{y_{rot}^2}{2\sigma_{minor}^2} \right] \right)$$

$$\begin{bmatrix} x_{rot} \\ y_{rot} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x - x_{tail} \\ y - y_{tail} \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\sigma_{major} = k_{speed} \cdot \Vert{}\vec{v}_{uav}\Vert{}, \quad \sigma_{minor} = w_{box} \cdot 0.3$$

Full 3D Navier-Stokes computational fluid dynamics (CFD) modeling of turbulent rotor wake thermal transport is computationally intractable during network training. An empirical 2D asymmetric Gaussian tail appended along the inverse velocity vector replicates the spatial heat signature of drone exhaust plumes in real time.

Deep trackers (e.g., SiamDT) often suffer from bounding box drift toward trailing thermal exhaust plumes rather than centering on the drone chassis. Augmenting images with synthetic thermal plumes forces regression heads to learn robust target center localization despite asymmetric heat trails.

### Dynamic Range Compression & AGC Quantization Artifacts

Uncooled TIR sensors capture high dynamic range (HDR) raw data (14-bit to 16-bit counts). Infrared image processing engines apply Automatic Gain Control (AGC) histogram equalization or plateau equalization algorithms to compress raw radiometric counts into 8-bit display outputs. When strong background heat sources (e.g., solar ground glint, hot buildings) enter the field of view, AGC algorithms compress target dynamic range, reducing target thermal contrast.

Plateau-limited histogram equalization maps high bit-depth input intensities $X$ to 8-bit dynamic range:

$$P(k) = \frac{\min\left(H(k), T_{plateau}\right)}{\sum_{j=0}^{N_{max}} \min\left(H(j), T_{plateau}\right)}$$

$$Y = \text{Floor}\left( (L_{max} - 1) \sum_{k=0}^{X} P(k) \right)$$

Simulated dynamic range compression is simplified by applying local intensity quantization combined with contrast binning across target search patches:

$$I_{quant}(x,y) = \text{Round}\left( \frac{I(x,y)}{Q_{step}} \right) \cdot Q_{step}$$

$$Q_{step} = \frac{255}{2^b}, \quad b \sim \text{Uniform}(3, 6)$$

Re-implementing proprietary hardware AGC pipelines (such as FLIR Linear or HEQ modes) requires raw 14-bit thermal data, whereas benchmark datasets are distributed in 8-bit format. Bit-depth truncation ($b \in [3, 6]$) reproduces the identical step-quantization and contrast loss caused by camera AGC shifts.

This prepares deep neural network feature extractors to retain discriminative object representations when targets enter low-contrast quantization bands during camera auto-gain recalibration.

## Differentiable GPU-Accelerated Pipeline Integration

To apply physical TIR augmentations efficiently during deep model training on Anti-UAV benchmarks, augmentations must be integrated directly into GPU batch pipelines using differentiable computer vision frameworks such as Kornia. Non-differentiable CPU data loaders (e.g., standard OpenCV or Pillow workflows) introduce severe CPU-GPU data transfer bottlenecks, reducing overall GPU compute utilization.

In a GPU-native pipeline, operations process 4D PyTorch image tensors allocated directly in CUDA memory. The pipeline first streams mini-batches into CUDA memory buffers, applying fixed-pattern noise (FPA stripe matrices) and scale-dependent Beer-Lambert intensity attenuation in parallel. Next, velocity vectors computed from sequential bounding box annotations parameterize directional motion blur kernels via Kornia spatial convolutions. Concurrently, dynamic spatial deformations update target bounding box coordinates within the PyTorch computation graph. Finally, bit-depth quantization and local AGC contrast binning are applied prior to passing the augmented tensors directly to tracker backbones (such as SiamDT or MixFormerV2).

Because transformations are formulated as differentiable tensor functions, backpropagation can pass through augmentation layers. This enables adversarial data augmentation strategies (such as spatial transformer network tuning), where augmentation parameters ($\beta_{ext}, NETD, \sigma_{NUC}$) are actively optimized to generate challenging training samples.

## Strategic Conclusions and Operational Implementation Pathways

Systematic evaluation reveals that standard RGB image augmentations fail to represent the physical processes of thermal infrared image formation. Incorporating physical domain knowledge—including microbolometer non-uniformity drift, Beer-Lambert atmospheric extinction, integration-time motion blur, and AGC dynamic range quantization—directly addresses primary tracking failure modes on Anti-UAV benchmarks.

To maximize performance gain on the Anti-UAV410 dataset, augmentation execution should follow a multi-stage physical ordering:

1. **Sensor Level:** Apply microbolometer NUC drift matrices and $NETD$ temporal Gaussian noise.
2. **Atmospheric Level:** Apply scale-dependent Beer-Lambert attenuation and elastic refraction warping.
3. **Target Dynamics:** Apply velocity-aligned motion MTF blur and asymmetric exhaust plume tails.
4. **Processing Level:** Apply AGC quantization truncation and global environmental gain shifts.

Training deep trackers using a curriculum learning schedule—starting with baseline sensor noise and progressively increasing the probability of severe thermal crossover and atmospheric extinction—prevents early gradient instability while ensuring robust feature extraction under operational environmental degradation.
