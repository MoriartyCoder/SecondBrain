## Domain Context and Technical Challenges in Thermal Infrared UAV Perception

Ground-to-air detection and tracking of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) using Thermal Infrared (TIR) computer vision represents a foundational capability in modern airspace monitoring, counter-drone interception, and defensive situational awareness. Unlike visible-spectrum (RGB) imaging sensors, long-wave thermal infrared sensors capture raw radiative emissions generated by target propulsion systems, internal electronics, and friction against atmospheric airflows. This radiative sensing capacity enables unconstrained surveillance across lightless nighttime conditions, overcast weather, thermal shadows, and smoke-obscured environments where visual sensors suffer total optical degradation. However, long-wave thermal imaging introduces severe perceptual noise characteristics and physical sensor artifacts that fundamentally alter the feature extraction dynamics of deep Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs), including standard Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers (ViTs).

Thermal infrared imagery inherently lacks rich chromaticity, fine surface texture maps, high-frequency structural contours, and color boundary cues. Instead, TIR frames exhibit narrow dynamic range distributions, reduced signal-to-noise ratios (SNR), pronounced sensor blurring, non-uniformity fixed-pattern noise (FPN), and spatial halo effects caused by intense localized heat radiation. In aerial drone perception, these physical sensor limitations produce four interconnected operational challenges that degrade object detection and single-object tracking performance:

Thermal Crossover occurs when an airborne drone operates against backgrounds with matching radiant intensity, such as sun-warmed cloud edges, concrete urban structures, or heat-absorbing canopy leaves. In these conditions, target visual contrast drops near zero, causing object boundaries to blend into surrounding background pixels.

Extreme Scale Variation and Tiny Target Collapse present severe spatial representation challenges. At typical defensive operational ranges exceeding several hundred meters, micro- or mini-UAVs occupy small spatial footprints, often accounting for less than 0.03% of the total frame area (frequently under $16 \times 16$ pixels, matching or falling below the optical Point Spread Function limit). Standard deep convolutional or hierarchical transformer backbones repeatedly apply strided pooling and spatial down-sampling operations. Without specialized high-resolution feature paths or adaptive spatial processing, these architectural operations can completely erase tiny target feature maps.

Dynamic Background Clutter and Unconstrained Target Absence complicate long-term target tracking. Low-altitude airspaces contain dynamic background interference, including wind-blown vegetation, moving ground vehicles, atmospheric cloud movements, and non-target thermal distractors such as birds or fixed-wing aircraft. Furthermore, real-world deployment requires detectors and trackers to infer target presence continuous frame-by-frame, outputting empty state flags when drones exit the field of view or undergo complete physical occlusion, and re-instantiating tracks upon target re-entry without generating persistent false positives.

IR Sensor Degradation and Environmental Effects include atmospheric Mie scattering, thermal blooming around motors, optical Point Spread Function (PSF) blur, and Automatic Gain Control (AGC) shifts. These sensor-level effects cause non-linear intensity scaling, target edge jitter, and signal attenuation.

To prevent deep neural network architectures from over-fitting to static environmental backgrounds or narrow thermal intensity distributions, domain-specific image data augmentation that models physical IR sensor effects is essential. Data augmentation strategies artificially expand the training distribution, enforcing spatial, structural, intensity, thermal, and temporal invariance. Analyzing the quantitative gains achieved by these strategies across standard infrared benchmarks—specifically Anti-UAV300, Anti-UAV410, and Anti-UAV600—reveals key principles for designing real-time, robust counter-UAV vision systems.

## Architectural Analysis of Benchmark Datasets: Anti-UAV300, Anti-UAV410, and Anti-UAV600

Evaluating anti-UAV detection and tracking models requires specialized benchmarks that reflect real-world operational challenges. The Anti-UAV dataset suite serves as the primary benchmark family for evaluating vision-based counter-drone algorithms, evolving from early dual-modality setups to large-scale, monocular thermal tracking benchmarks.

The original Anti-UAV benchmark, commonly designated as Anti-UAV300 or Anti-UAV318, established standardized evaluation protocols for ground-to-air drone tracking. The benchmark comprises 318 synchronized video pairs containing over 585,900 manually annotated bounding boxes captured simultaneously across visible-light ($1920 \times 1080$ resolution at 25 FPS) and thermal infrared ($640 \times 512$ resolution at 25 FPS) modalities. Anti-UAV300 introduced a dual-state existence protocol: when a target leaves the camera view or undergoes total occlusion, the ground-truth bounding box transitions to an empty frame accompanied by an explicit existence flag ($v_t = 0$). This forces neural networks to evaluate target existence alongside spatial localization.

Recognizing that long-wave infrared sensors serve as the primary operational modality during low-visibility and nighttime operations, the Anti-UAV410 benchmark focuses exclusively on monocular thermal infrared sequences. Anti-UAV410 expands dataset diversity to wild, unconstrained low-altitude scenarios. The dataset comprises 410 thermal video sequences containing over 438,000 manually annotated bounding boxes at $640 \times 512$ resolution. Anti-UAV410 incorporates frame-level attribute annotations across six environmental challenge vectors: Thermal Crossover (TC), Scale Variation (SV), Occlusion (OC), Out-of-View (OV), Dynamic Background Clutter (DBC), and Fast Motion (FM).

Addressing scale limitations in earlier datasets, the Anti-UAV600 benchmark expands the thermal evaluation suite to 600 infrared video sequences comprising over 723,000 densely annotated frames at $640 \times 512$ resolution. Anti-UAV600 introduces unannounced, random target presence and absence cycles across extended video timelines. Drones routinely enter, leave, and re-enter camera views across complex background environments including cloudy skies, dense urban buildings, forest canopies, and mountain ridges. This structure tests an algorithm's ability to maintain long-term tracking stability while minimizing false-positive tracks during target absence.

Complementing this benchmark series is the CST Anti-UAV dataset, designed specifically to isolate tiny target tracking performance. CST Anti-UAV provides 220 thermal sequences with 240,000 frames, containing 78,224 annotated tiny target instances where drones occupy minimal spatial areas—roughly 4.5 times more tiny object instances than standard thermal tracking benchmarks.

## Taxonomy and Mechanisms of Infrared Data Augmentation Strategies

Data augmentation strategies for thermal infrared drone perception fall into six main technical categories: physical IR sensor effects modeling, spectrum-guided intensity mapping, spatial slicing, composite sample generation, synthetic environmental degradation, and offline structural feature modulation.

### Physical IR Sensor Effects and Thermal Radiative Modeling

Traditional RGB data augmentations (such as random hue adjustments, saturation tweaks, or sharp color channel swapping) disrupt the physical structure of thermal images because thermal sensors measure single-channel radiometric emission rather than visible reflected light. Modern IR data augmentation frameworks incorporate physical sensor degradation and thermal radiation models directly into the pipeline:

Thermal Blooming and Radiance Halo Simulation: High-temperature components of drones (e.g., electric motors, electronic speed controllers, exhaust headers) emit strong radiative flux, causing thermal saturation in focal plane array (FPA) detectors. This saturation manifests as thermal blooming—a localized high-intensity blur or halo effect extending beyond the physical target boundary. Augmenting images with Gaussian-weighted intensity expansion around target boundaries simulates thermal blooming, forcing neural network localization heads to remain robust against halo-induced bounding box expansion.

Point Spread Function (PSF) and Thermal Optical Blur Injection: Long-wave infrared camera lenses exhibit diffraction and aberration characteristics governed by the optical Point Spread Function (PSF). When drones operate at long distances or experience rapid relative motion, the target image blurs across adjacent sensor pixels. Injecting dynamic isotropic and anisotropic Gaussian spatial blur matrices models physical optical PSF limits and high-speed motion blur, improving model robustness against target boundary blurring.

Fixed-Pattern Noise (FPN) and Sensor Thermal Noise: Thermal infrared FPAs suffer from non-uniform response across individual detector elements, creating non-uniformity Fixed-Pattern Noise (FPN) and high-frequency thermal temporal noise. Applying zero-mean Gaussian noise matrices and spatial grid gain variations during training replicates low Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) operating environments ($1 \le \text{SNR} \le 3$), teaching detection backbones to separate genuine thermal target signatures from sensor noise artifacts.

### Spectrum-Guided Intensity Transformations

Spectrum-guided augmentations alter intensity profiles using non-linear transformations such as Sinusoidal IR Mapping. This technique modifies pixel intensity distributions using a periodic sine function that compresses, expands, or alternates thermal contrast levels while maintaining spatial continuity:

$$y = \frac{\sin(2\pi x + \theta) + 1}{2}$$

where $x \in [0, 1]$ represents the normalized input pixel intensity, $y \in [0, 1]$ is the transformed output intensity, and $\theta \in [0, 2\pi]$ is a randomized phase shift applied consistently across spatial dimensions.

Varying the phase shift $\theta$ during training simulates real-world thermal variations, including diurnal temperature shifts, atmospheric humidity changes, sensor Automatic Gain Control (AGC) adjustments, and thermal crossover events. This forces feature extractors to rely on structural and morphological target cues rather than static grayscale intensity ranges.

### Spatial Slicing and Geometric Re-sampling

Detecting small targets often fails when spatial feature map dimensions contract within deep neural network backbones. Tiling strategies address this by cropping high-resolution thermal frames into overlapping sub-images centered around candidate target regions. These cropped tiles are then resampled to the network's nominal input resolution.

This resampling increases the relative pixel footprint of small targets. As a result, early convolutional layers and self-attention blocks can extract discriminative structural features before spatial down-sampling occurs.

### Composite and Mixing Augmentations

Composite augmentation techniques combine multiple training images to increase target diversity and teach models to recognize partially occluded objects:

Mosaic Augmentation stitches four distinct training images into a single composite frame. This forces the model to locate targets across varied spatial contexts and scaled frame borders, reducing reliance on global scene structure and improving localization near image boundaries.

CutMix Augmentation extracts rectangular regions from one thermal image and overlays them onto another frame, adjusting target labels proportionally. In thermal drone detection, CutMix forces neural networks to identify targets using partial visual cues, preventing dependence on any single sub-component.

Copy-Paste Augmentation isolates drone target masks and inserts them onto diverse background scenes containing false-positive distractors, such as birds or cloud clutter. This breaks spurious correlations between static background context and target presence.

### Synthetic Environmental Degradation

Atmospheric phenomena like fog, haze, and rain degrade thermal radiation propagation through Mie scattering, attenuating target contrast and blurring structural edges. Synthetic environmental degradation models simulate atmospheric fog using optical depth equations:

$$I(x) = J(x)e^{-\beta d(x)} + L_\infty \left(1 - e^{-\beta d(x)}\right)$$

where $I(x)$ represents the degraded thermal frame, $J(x)$ is the clear-sky radiance, $d(x)$ is the estimated scene depth, $L_\infty$ is atmospheric light, and $\beta$ is the scattering coefficient. Training models across a continuum of synthetic fog levels ($\beta \in [0.05, 0.50]$) builds robustness against weather-induced performance loss.

### Structural Feature Modulation

Rather than modifying raw pixel inputs, structural augmentation strategies alter feature maps directly within the network architecture during training. The Dual-Flow Semantic Consistency (DFSC) strategy extracts Region of Interest (ROI) features from cross-sequence query images and uses them to modulate search frame feature maps during training.

This feature-level modulation exposes classification and regression heads to cross-sequence target variations. Because semantic modulation operates strictly during training, it improves generalization without adding inference latency during deployment.

## Quantitative Performance Evaluation Across Neural Architectures and Benchmarks

The quantitative impact of image data augmentation varies based on the underlying neural network architecture. Evaluating performance metrics across Convolutional Neural Networks (e.g., YOLOv5, YOLOv8, YOLOv11) and Vision Transformers (e.g., RT-DETR, Swin-Transformer backbones) demonstrates the effectiveness of these augmentation techniques across thermal Anti-UAV benchmarks.

### Convolutional Neural Network Detection Frameworks

Lightweight object detectors optimized for edge hardware rely heavily on spatial and composite data augmentations. Fine-tuning a COCO-pretrained YOLOv8n detector on the Anti-UAV dataset yields a $13.6\times$ performance increase over un-tuned baselines, achieving an $83.2\%\text{ }mAP_{50-95}$ and $99.5\%\text{ }mAP_{50}$ at 95 FPS on TensorRT FP16 edge engines. On the DUT Anti-UAV dataset, integrating Bidirectional Separable Convolution (BSC) alongside scale-aware augmentations into YOLOv8s raises $mAP_{50}$ to 91.7% (+1.5% over standard YOLOv8s) while operating at 77.3 FPS.

Lightweight architectures like YOLOv11-mini demonstrate strong performance gains from composite data augmentations. Without augmentation, YOLOv11-mini struggles with tiny target recall and precision. Applying a combined pipeline of Medium intensity scaling, Mosaic, and CutMix increases $mAP_{50}$ to 90.7% and Precision to 96.3%. Mosaic augmentation provides the single largest gain in $mAP_{50-95}$ (+0.49), showing its importance in teaching spatial scale invariance to lightweight backbones.

Under atmospheric degradation testing on Anti-UAV300 using YOLOv5m, models trained exclusively on clear-sky images degrade significantly when exposed to synthetic fog, dropping from $0.956\text{ }mAP_{50}$ under clear conditions to $0.048\text{ }mAP_{50}$ under heavy fog ($\beta = 0.50$). Integrating fog-aware synthetic degradation during training maintains performance under heavy fog at $0.829\text{ }mAP_{50}$—representing a $+0.320\text{ }mAP_{50-95}$ improvement at moderate fog levels. This environmental robustness incurs a minor 9.1% clear-sky performance trade-off ($mAP_{50-95}$ shifts from 0.567 to 0.515).

### Vision Transformer and Siamese Tracking Architectures

Vision Transformers (ViTs) and Detection Transformers (DETRs) lack the local inductive bias inherent to convolutional filters. Consequently, transformer architectures rely heavily on data volume and effective augmentation strategies.

Evaluating RT-DETR and YOLOv8m under IR-guided Sinusoidal Spectrum Augmentation highlights this architectural difference. While YOLOv8m exhibits consistent 3% to 5% improvements across $mAP_{50}$, Precision, and Recall, RT-DETR gains 5% to 6% across the same metrics when sinusoidal mapping is applied. Sinusoidal intensity mapping regularizes transformer attention heads, preventing them from over-fitting to narrow intensity ranges and encouraging them to learn broader structural features.

In multi-modal and transformer-based detection, architectures designed for tiny thermal target representation achieve high accuracy benchmarks:

DCAM-DETR uses deformable cross-attention mechanisms on Anti-UAV300, achieving a state-of-the-art $94.7\%\text{ }mAP_{50}$ at 42 FPS.

CSFADet uses cross-scale feature adaptation to achieve $91.4\%\text{ }mAP_{50}$ on Anti-UAV300, maintaining performance during low-resolution (LR) and thermal crossover (TC) events.

For single-object tracking (SOT) on Anti-UAV410 and Anti-UAV600, Siamese frameworks using Swin-Transformer backbones benefit from structural and scale augmentations:

The SiamDT baseline combines a Swin-Transformer backbone with semantic feature enhancement on Anti-UAV410, establishing a baseline State Accuracy (SA) of 66.76% and Precision of 86.40%.

Enhanced-SiamDT incorporates multi-scale spatial refinement and attribute-specific augmentations, raising State Accuracy to 68.58% (+1.82 percentage points) and Precision to 89.92% (+3.52 percentage points) on Anti-UAV410.

OSGT (One-Stage Global Tracker) embeds scale-adaptive modules (SAEM) and applies Gaussian noise perturbations during training, outperforming SiamDT on Anti-UAV410 by +0.79% SA.

MDTC (Motion-Detection Tracking Collaboration) combines an improved YOLOv8s detector with a motion verification module on Anti-UAV600, achieving an IoU of 0.618, State Accuracy (ACC) of 0.544, and a Tracking Success Rate (SR) of 0.802.

## Architectural Asymmetries, Environmental Trade-offs, and Theoretical Insights

Comparing data augmentation strategies across neural network architectures and thermal benchmarks highlights three key technical dynamics:

Architectural Asymmetry in Augmentation Response reflects differences in how CNNs and Vision Transformers process spatial data. Convolutional networks use localized receptive fields, providing built-in spatial inductive biases. Consequently, CNNs benefit most from geometric and composite augmentations like Mosaic and CutMix, which force the network to extract localized target features independently of global frame positions.

In contrast, Vision Transformers rely on global self-attention mechanisms, making them susceptible to over-fitting on small thermal datasets. Without localized spatial biases, transformers can over-fit to specific pixel intensity ranges or background textures. Spectrum-guided techniques, such as Sinusoidal IR Mapping, address this by randomizing non-linear intensity distributions while maintaining continuous spatial structures. This forces self-attention mechanisms to learn structural and topological target representations, producing larger relative accuracy gains on transformer models like RT-DETR (+5% to +6%) than on CNNs (+3% to +5%).

The Clear-Sky vs. Environmental Robustness Trade-off represents an operational compromise when training under synthetic degradation. Models trained exclusively on clear-sky frames achieve high precision under optimal conditions but degrade significantly when exposed to atmospheric scattering. Incorporating fog-aware synthetic degradation during training causes a minor 9.1% drop in clear-sky $mAP_{50-95}$ on Anti-UAV300 (from 0.567 to 0.515). However, this trade-off prevents complete detection failure under adverse weather, maintaining an $mAP_{50}$ of 0.829 under moderate fog where clear-trained models fail. In operational deployments, accepting this slight reduction in clear-weather performance is necessary to ensure consistent system reliability across varying weather conditions.

Mitigating Spatial Information Collapse in Tiny Target Regimes is critical for long-range detection. Drones at long distances frequently occupy fewer than $16 \times 16$ pixels, accounting for less than 0.03% of the image area. Standard down-sampling operations in deep backbones can erase these tiny target representations. Applying aggressive cropping or down-sampling can inadvertently remove or blur these targets. To preserve tiny target features, augmentation pipelines must combine tiling with scale-aware noise injection. Tiling preserves target resolution relative to the network input size, while noise injection regularizes bounding box regression heads. This combination improves tracking stability during thermal crossover events, ensuring the network retains distinct target representations even when target contrast approaches background levels.

## Strategic Recommendations and Deployment Frameworks for Counter-UAV Systems

Optimizing deep neural networks for thermal infrared drone detection requires tailoring data augmentation strategies to the model architecture, sensor specifications, and operational environment:

For Lightweight Edge CNNs (such as YOLOv8n and YOLOv11-mini deployed on embedded hardware), use composite augmentations combining Mosaic, CutMix, and Copy-Paste alongside physical noise injection. These techniques improve spatial feature extraction and localized context learning without adding computational overhead during inference.

For Server-Class Vision Transformers (such as RT-DETR and Swin-Transformer trackers), apply spectrum-guided Sinusoidal IR Mapping paired with spatial Tiling and Thermal Blooming Simulation. Sinusoidal mapping regularizes self-attention mechanisms against thermal contrast variations, while tiling preserves positional embeddings for tiny targets.

For Systems Operating in Variable Climates, integrate synthetic environmental degradation—specifically fog modeling and thermal contrast decay—directly into the training pipeline. Training across a continuum of synthetic fog levels ($\beta = 0.05 \text{ to } 0.30$) builds operational robustness against atmospheric scattering and adverse weather conditions.

For Multi-Object and Single-Object Trackers, incorporate training-time structural feature modulations like Dual-Flow Semantic Consistency (DFSC) and sensor Point Spread Function blur matrices. Modulating search feature maps with cross-sequence target features during training improves tracker resilience against occlusion, thermal crossover, and target edge blurring without increasing operational inference latency.
